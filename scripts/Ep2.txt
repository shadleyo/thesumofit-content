Episode two. Interest works both ways. Welcome to The Sum of It.

Chapter one. The same maths, pointed at you.

In episode one, compounding made savings grow: interest earning interest of its own. Debt runs on exactly the same maths. The only difference is who it works for.

At twenty-two percent a year, a five thousand dollar balance adds about ninety-two dollars of interest in a single month. Pay less than that, and the debt grows, even though you paid.

And if you stop paying altogether? At twenty-two percent, compounded monthly, five thousand dollars becomes seven thousand, seven hundred in two years. In five years, it's almost fifteen thousand. Nearly triple, without spending another cent.

Chapter two. The minimum payment trap.

A common minimum payment is the month's interest, plus one percent of the balance. On five thousand dollars, that starts at about one hundred and forty-two dollars.

But as the balance falls, so does the minimum. Each month you pay a little less, so each month the balance falls a little slower. That's how a five thousand dollar debt stretches to nineteen years.

The minimum is designed to keep you current. It isn't designed to get you out.

Chapter three. Fix the payment.

The simplest move: pick a fixed amount, and keep paying it as the balance falls.

Paying the minimum: nineteen years, and eight thousand, one hundred dollars of interest. A fixed one hundred and fifty: under four and a half years, and two thousand, eight hundred. Two hundred and fifty a month: just over two years, and under thirteen hundred. Five hundred a month: one year, and five hundred and seventy-four dollars.

Every extra dollar goes straight to the balance, and stops interest growing on it.

Chapter four. Not all debt costs the same.

Borrowing one thousand dollars for a year at seven percent, a rate many car loans charge, costs about seventy dollars. At twenty-two percent on a credit card, about two hundred and twenty. On a twenty-nine percent store card, about two hundred and ninety.

The rate is the price of the money. Know the price of every debt you have.

And watch for zero percent offers. Zero percent for twelve months sounds free, and it can be, if you clear the balance in time. But if three thousand dollars is still there when the offer ends, and the rate jumps to twenty-two percent, the next year adds about seven hundred and thirty dollars of interest. Mark the end date, and divide the balance by the months you have.

Chapter five. Which debt first?

Say you owe three debts. Three thousand on a card at twenty-two percent. Fifteen hundred on another card at eighteen. And eight thousand on a car loan at seven. The minimums come to three hundred and thirty-five dollars a month.

Pay only the minimums, and you're paying for over four years, with three thousand, four hundred dollars of interest.

Now find an extra three hundred a month, and point it at one debt at a time.

The avalanche method: highest rate first. Everything is paid off in twenty-two months, with one thousand, one hundred and sixty-three dollars of interest.

The snowball method: smallest balance first. Also twenty-two months, with one thousand, two hundred and three dollars of interest. Forty dollars more. But the first debt is gone in five months, instead of nine.

The avalanche costs less. The snowball gives you a win sooner. Either one beats the minimums by more than two thousand dollars.

Chapter six. Pay it off, or invest?

Paying off a twenty-two percent debt is like earning twenty-two percent, guaranteed. In episode one, we used seven percent for investing, and even that isn't guaranteed.

So for high-interest debt, paying it down usually wins. But keep a small emergency fund first, so the next surprise doesn't go straight back on the card.

Chapter seven. Three things to take away.

One. Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum.

Two. Highest rate first saves the most. Smallest first, if you need the early win.

Three. Every dollar of debt you clear is a guaranteed return.

Interest works both ways. Make it work for you. That's the sum of it.
