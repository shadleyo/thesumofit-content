Let's start with the strangest idea in money. Imagine someone offers you a choice: a million dollars today, or a single penny that doubles every day for thirty days.

Most people take the million. For three weeks, they look clever. On day twenty, the penny is worth just five thousand two hundred dollars. Then something happens. It passes the million on day twenty-eight. On day thirty, it's worth five point three seven million. And half of that arrived on the very last day.

Real investments don't double every day. A long-run stock market average is closer to seven percent a year, and some years it falls. But the shape of the curve is the same. Slow. Slow. Then sudden. That's compounding: growth earning growth of its own.

Here's the key part. When you put money in, the deposit just sits there. It doesn't multiply. What multiplies is the return. In year one, your return is small, because there isn't much to grow. But every year, the pile it's working on gets bigger.

Take two hundred dollars a month for forty years at seven percent. You put in ninety-six thousand dollars. You end up with about five hundred and twenty-five thousand. So where did the other four hundred and twenty-nine thousand come from? Growth. Not your deposits. And look at when it shows up. For the first eighteen years, your deposits are the bigger part of the pile. After that, growth takes over, and it never gives the lead back.

Which brings us to the most expensive habit in personal finance. Waiting.

Same two hundred dollars a month, same seven percent, retiring at sixty-five. Start at twenty-five, and you end up with five hundred and twenty-five thousand. Wait one year, and you end up with four hundred and eighty-seven thousand. You skipped just two thousand four hundred dollars of deposits, and it cost you thirty-seven thousand. Wait five years, and the gap is one hundred and sixty-five thousand. Wait ten, and it's two hundred and eighty-one thousand. More than half of what you could have had.

Every year you wait doesn't remove a year from the start of your savings. It removes a year from the end. And the end is where the biggest years live.

Now, maybe you're thinking: I can't spare two hundred dollars a month. Fair enough. So let's go smaller.

Time isn't the only lever. The rate matters too. Here's a five second trick: the rule of seventy-two. Divide seventy-two by your yearly return, and you get roughly how many years it takes your money to double. At four percent, about eighteen years. At seven percent, about ten. At ten percent, roughly seven.

That's why one percentage point matters so much. Invest three hundred dollars a month for forty years. At seven percent, you end up with seven hundred and eighty-seven thousand. At eight percent, over one million and forty thousand. One point. Two hundred and sixty thousand dollars.

And it works in reverse. A fund charging two percent a year instead of a quarter of a percent can quietly take more than a quarter of your final balance over thirty years. In our example, that was ninety-eight thousand dollars. Fees compound too.

Now, the obvious objection. What if the market crashes? It will. Over forty years, you should expect several big falls. And losses hurt more than gains help: if an investment drops fifty percent, it has to rise one hundred percent just to get back to where it started.

But here's what that means for someone saving every month. When prices fall, the same two hundred dollars buys more. Those cheap purchases are often the ones that grow the most when markets recover. The seven percent in these examples is a long-run average that includes the bad years. Time doesn't remove the drops. It gives you enough runway to recover from them. That's also why money you'll need in the next few years usually doesn't belong in the stock market at all.

There's one more lever that's easy to miss: free money. Many employers will match part of what you save for retirement. Say you earn fifty thousand dollars, save five percent, and your employer matches it. Over thirty years at seven percent, your own contributions grow to about two hundred and fifty-four thousand dollars. With the match, it's about five hundred and eight thousand. The same effort, doubled, before the market does anything. If your employer offers a match and you're not taking it, that's the first thing to look at.

So, how much would you actually need to put away to reach a million dollars by sixty-five? It depends almost entirely on when you start. Start at twenty-five, and it's three hundred and eighty-one dollars a month. Start at thirty-five, and it's eight hundred and twenty. Start at forty-five, and it's one thousand nine hundred and twenty. Every ten years you wait more than doubles the monthly bill.

If you're reading those numbers at forty, or fifty, and feeling like you've missed it, you haven't. The best time to start was yesterday. The second best time is today. Waiting one more year still costs more than any year before it, so the maths always says the same thing: begin now, with whatever you can, and let time do the heavy lifting.

Three things to take away. One: deposits add, returns multiply. Two: waiting removes your biggest years, not your smallest. Three: small amounts, low fees and a long runway beat big amounts started late.
